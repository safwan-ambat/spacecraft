## The room now has two audiences

For decades, a meeting room was designed for the people inside it. The table, the chairs and a projector screen served everyone who had walked in. Hybrid work has added a second audience: the colleagues, clients or partners who join through a laptop in another city, at home or on a train platform.

These remote participants experience the room through a camera and a microphone. If they cannot see faces, they cannot read reactions. If they cannot hear the person at the far end of the table, they stop contributing. A room that works well for those present but poorly for those joining remotely quietly excludes half the meeting.

Designing for hybrid meetings means treating the remote participant as a full member of the room. That changes where the camera goes, how sound is captured, what the display shows and how the furniture is arranged.

## Put the camera and screen at eye level

The most common mistake is mounting a camera high on the wall, or above a screen that is itself mounted high. Remote participants then look down onto the tops of heads and see people looking away from them, towards the screen.

Place the camera as close as possible to seated eye level, ideally just above or just below the display that shows remote colleagues. When people look at the faces on the screen, they then appear to be looking at the camera too. In larger rooms, consider a camera with automatic framing that follows the speaker or frames the group, so that remote participants are not left watching an empty end of the table.

Check the camera's field of view against the seating plan. Every chair should be visible. A camera that crops out the two seats closest to the screen leaves those people invisible to anyone joining remotely.

## Capture every voice

Sound matters more than video. People will tolerate a blurred image but not missing words.

A single microphone built into a screen or speaker bar works for a small room. In larger rooms, ceiling microphones or table microphones spaced along the table provide even coverage, so that the person at the far end is heard as clearly as the person beside the screen. Test coverage from every seat before the room is handed over.

The room's acoustics affect this too. Hard glass walls and bare floors create echo, which microphones pick up and amplify. Soft finishes on walls and ceiling, a rug and upholstered chairs reduce reverberation and make speech clearer for everyone. Keep air-conditioning noise low, and position supply grilles away from microphones.

A display that shows a shared presentation clearly may be too small to show six remote participants at a readable scale. Faces should appear close to life size from the furthest seat, if possible.

Two displays side by side, one for content and one for faces, can resolve this in larger rooms. In smaller rooms, a single large display often suffices if the video platform arranges content and faces sensibly. Mount displays so their centre sits near seated eye level, not high on the wall.

## Choose a layout for hybrid meetings

The traditional long table with a screen at one end places remote participants at the head of the table, which can work. But it also means people at the far end are small in the camera's view and far from the screen.

Two alternatives are now common:

- A front-of-room layout, where a tapered or V-shaped table opens towards the display wall, so everyone in the room faces the camera and remote participants together.
- A shared-wall layout, where the table runs parallel to a long wall carrying the display and camera, and in-room participants sit along one side, facing it. This works well for smaller groups and gives remote attendees a clear, even view of everyone.

Both approaches give up some of the symmetry of a conventional boardroom in exchange for a more equal meeting.

## Light faces, not tabletops

Cameras need faces to be evenly and softly lit. Downlights directly overhead cast shadows under eyes and noses; a bright window behind participants turns them into silhouettes on screen.

Add soft light from the front, for example from wall washers on the display wall or diffused fittings in the ceiling forward of the table. Control daylight with blinds so that the camera is not looking into a bright window. Keep colour temperature consistent so that faces do not look grey or orange on screen.

## Set rules for booking and behaviour

Technology alone does not make hybrid meetings fair. Simple conventions help. Start the video call from the room system rather than individual laptops, to avoid echo. Ask in-room participants to speak towards the room microphone rather than into their own devices. Invite remote attendees to speak first in discussions, since they cannot interrupt as easily.

Booking matters too. When rooms are scarce, people book large rooms for solo calls. A booking system that shows room capacity and suggests smaller spaces for one or two people keeps the larger rooms free for group meetings.

## Let small rooms absorb most calls

Most hybrid meetings involve one or two people in the office and several elsewhere. These do not need a meeting room for eight. They need a quiet, well-lit enclosure with a decent camera and a good microphone.

Phone booths, one-person focus rooms and two-person rooms now carry most of the call load in many offices. Plan for enough of them, distributed across the floor so that nobody has to walk far. Give each good ventilation, since a closed booth warms quickly, and a simple, consistent setup that anyone can use without help.

A useful first question is how many calls are taken each day, and from where. The answer tends to show that the most important meeting space is no longer the boardroom, but the small room nearest to the desk.
