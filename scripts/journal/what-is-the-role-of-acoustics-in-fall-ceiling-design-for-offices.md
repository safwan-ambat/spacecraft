## Look up before you look around

In most offices the ceiling is the one surface that is uninterrupted. Walls are broken by doors, glass and joinery. Floors disappear under desks and chairs. The ceiling spans the whole room, faces every speaker and receives a large share of the sound produced below it. Whatever it is made of has more influence on how a room sounds than any other single surface.

Yet ceiling decisions are often driven by other concerns: the height under the beams, the AC ducting, the lighting layout or the look of the reception. Acoustics appear late, if at all. Bringing them forward costs little; it needs only a clear idea of what a ceiling can do for sound.

## Two numbers that describe two jobs

Ceiling products are usually described by two acoustic measures. They answer different questions, and confusing them is the most common mistake in ceiling specification.

**NRC, the noise reduction coefficient**, describes how much sound striking the surface is absorbed rather than reflected. It is expressed on a scale from zero to one: a low figure means the ceiling behaves like a mirror for sound, a high figure means it takes most of the energy out. NRC governs the sound within a room, how loud the floor becomes and how long sound lingers.

**CAC, the ceiling attenuation class**, describes how well a ceiling stops sound from travelling up into the void above one room, along the plenum, and down into the next room. A higher figure means better blocking. CAC governs privacy between adjacent rooms that share a ceiling void.

The two tend to pull apart. Soft, porous materials absorb well but let sound pass through. Dense, heavy materials block well but reflect. Some products are designed to do a reasonable job of both, usually by combining a porous face with a denser backing. When comparing options, ask the supplier for both figures, tested to a named standard, and decide which matters more for each room.

## Comparing the common systems

Each ceiling type found in Indian offices brings a different acoustic character.

- **Mineral fibre tiles** in an exposed or semi-concealed grid are the standard choice for open workspaces. Many offer useful absorption, and they give easy access to services above. Check moisture resistance in humid cities and near pantries, because some tiles sag when the air stays damp through the monsoon.
- **Gypsum board** in a continuous plastered finish is smooth and clean but, in its plain form, reflects most sound. It blocks reasonably well. Perforated gypsum with an acoustic fleece behind it adds absorption while keeping the monolithic look, which suits receptions and boardrooms.
- **Metal ceilings**, in tiles, planks or linear strips, are durable and resist humidity and dust well. Plain metal is reflective; perforated metal with an acoustic infill behind it can absorb well.
- **Open ceilings with baffles or rafts** leave the slab exposed and suspend absorbing elements beneath it. They suit floors where height is tight or where the exposed structure is part of the design. They do nothing to stop sound passing between rooms, so they work best over open areas.
- **Wood and slatted systems** are mainly visual unless backed with an absorbing layer. With one, they can perform well.

## The leak above the partition

The most frequent acoustic complaint in a fitted office is that conversations in one cabin can be heard in the next. The partition between them may be well built and well rated. The sound is simply going over the top.

This is called flanking. Most office partitions stop at the underside of the false ceiling. Above it lies a continuous void running across the whole floor, carrying ducts, cables and sprinkler pipes. Sound passes up through the ceiling tile, travels along the void and drops through the tile in the next room. In effect, the partition is only as good as the ceiling it meets.

There are three ways to deal with it:

1. **Take the partition to the slab.** Running the wall through the ceiling void to the underside of the structure is the most reliable fix. Every service penetration must then be sealed, and the AC return-air route must be resolved so that rooms are not starved of air.
2. **Install a plenum barrier.** Where a full-height wall is impractical, a dense acoustic barrier can be fitted in the void directly above the partition line, sealed to the slab and around services.
3. **Use a high-CAC ceiling** across both rooms. This reduces the problem but rarely solves it for confidential rooms.

Before choosing, check the building management rules. Some tech parks restrict fixings into the slab or require approvals for anything that alters fire compartments or sprinkler coverage. Any change to partitions in the void should be confirmed against the approved fire plan and local fire requirements.

## Lights, grilles and the gaps between them

A ceiling is rarely just tiles. It carries light fittings, AC diffusers, return grilles, speakers, sensors and access panels. Each opening is a gap in its acoustic performance.

- Return-air grilles are open holes into the plenum. In a private room, a lined return-air path or a cross-talk attenuator keeps the duct from becoming a speaking tube between rooms.
- Recessed lights with open backs let sound through; ask whether fittings can be supplied with closed housings for sensitive rooms.
- Cassette units in a VRV system and indoor split units bring their own fan noise. Ask for the sound rating of the indoor unit at its operating speed, not only at its lowest setting.
- Coordinate one reflected ceiling plan for all trades. When lighting, AC and fire contractors each mark their own positions, the result is a ceiling full of cut tiles and gaps.

## Matching the ceiling to the room

As a working guide, an open workspace benefits most from high absorption. A meeting room or cabin needs a balance of absorption and good blocking, with walls taken to the slab or a barrier above. A boardroom used for video calls needs controlled reflections so that microphones pick up voices clearly. Corridors and pantries need durable, cleanable surfaces that still absorb some sound.

## Ask the right questions early

Before the ceiling layout is frozen, ask:

- Which rooms need privacy from their neighbours, and how will flanking above partitions be stopped?
- What are the NRC and CAC values of the proposed products, and to which standard were they tested?
- How will the ceiling cope with monsoon humidity and dust over the length of the lease?
- Where will return air travel, and does that route connect private rooms?
- Has every opening been coordinated on one reflected ceiling plan?

A ceiling chosen with these answers in hand will do much of the acoustic work in an office quietly, without ever being noticed.
