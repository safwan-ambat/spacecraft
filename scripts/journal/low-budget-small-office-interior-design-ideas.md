## Begin with the working day

A small office with a modest budget cannot afford to spend evenly. It has to choose. The most reliable way to choose is to look at what people actually do for eight or nine hours, and to put money where that experience is shaped most directly.

For most small teams, the answer is the same handful of things: the chair they sit in, the light they work under, the ability to take a call without being overheard, and a reliable place to plug in. Everything else, from feature walls to bespoke joinery, matters less than it first appears. Start the budget with these four, and allocate what remains afterwards.

## Spend on the chair

A person sits in an office chair for most of the working day. No other object in the office receives that much use or has as much effect on comfort. A good task chair adjusts in seat height, seat depth, back angle and arm height, and it lasts long enough to be moved to the next office.

This is one place to resist the cheapest option. A modest desk with a good chair is a better workplace than a handsome desk with a poor one. If the budget will not stretch to the chair you want for everyone, buy fewer, better chairs for the people at their desks all day, and simpler seating for meeting tables and occasional use.

## Spend on light

Light is the second priority, and it is often already half solved. Begin by understanding the daylight you have. Keep desks near the windows, keep tall furniture and partitions away from the glass, and choose blinds that cut glare without darkening the room. In a hot climate, a west-facing window will need more control than a north-facing one.

Where artificial light is needed, the fittings matter less than their placement and quality. Even, glare-free ceiling light with a neutral colour, supplemented by a few desk lamps for those who want them, will improve a small office more than decorative pendants. If the existing ceiling has old fittings in a sound grid, replacing the lamps or panels with efficient LED equivalents can be enough.

## Spend on one quiet room

Small offices are often open plan by necessity, and open plan is noisy. Video calls have made the problem worse. Rather than treating every surface acoustically, invest properly in one enclosed room where a person or two can speak without disturbing others and without being overheard.

Do that room well. A solid door with seals, a partition that runs to the slab or is properly treated above the ceiling, absorbent material on at least two walls, and decent lighting for cameras. One good room is more useful than three rooms built with thin glass and gaps at the top. In the open area, a rug, soft seating and a pinboard wall will take the edge off the noise at little cost.

## Spend on power and data

Running new power and data later, through a finished office, is disruptive and costly. Run it properly at the start. Give each workstation enough sockets for a laptop, a monitor and a phone charger, and place floor or desk-level outlets where people will sit rather than along the walls where furniture will hide them.

Confirm what is on backup. In many Indian buildings, the landlord's DG covers lighting and some sockets but not all. A small UPS for the network equipment and key desks keeps the office working through short outages. Put the router and switches somewhere ventilated and accessible, not in a sealed cupboard.

## Where to save

Saving money is easier once the priorities are protected. The following are sensible places to hold back:

- **Keep the existing ceiling and air conditioning** if they work. Moving ducts, diffusers and sprinkler heads is among the most expensive changes in a small fit-out. Plan rooms around what is already there.
- **Paint rather than replace.** Dated wood or laminate panelling can often be primed and painted. A single calm colour across walls and existing joinery will unify a space more effectively than new cladding.
- **Choose off-the-shelf furniture.** Standard desks, storage and tables from established manufacturers are cheaper, quicker and easier to add to than custom pieces. Save bespoke joinery for one element that matters, such as a reception counter or pantry.
- **Build fewer full-height partitions.** Each wall from floor to slab brings lighting, air conditioning, fire and electrical changes with it. Use furniture, shelving and screens to define areas instead.
- **Keep the flooring** if it is sound. A deep clean, a repair to damaged sections and a few well-placed rugs can carry an office for years.

## Phase what remains

A small budget does not need to deliver a finished office on the first day. It needs to deliver a working one. Decide what must be present at move-in and what can follow once the team has used the space for a few months.

Artwork, planting, branded graphics, a second meeting room, soft seating in the pantry: these can all come later, and often they are better for waiting. The team will know by then which corners are underused and which settings are missing. Keep a short list of these second-phase items, with rough priorities, and return to it at a natural point in the year.

The method is open to anyone. Spend on what people touch, sit in and work under. Save on what the building already provides. Leave room to finish the rest once the office has shown you what it needs.
