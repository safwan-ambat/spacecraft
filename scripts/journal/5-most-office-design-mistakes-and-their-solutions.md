## Early decisions, long consequences

Most office design mistakes are not failures of taste. They are failures of sequence: a question that should have been answered early was left until it was too late, or answered with an assumption rather than evidence. The result looks fine in photographs and frustrates the people who use it every day.

Five such mistakes recur often enough to be worth naming. Each is made at the start of a project, and each is far cheaper to avoid than to correct.

## Designing for headcount instead of attendance

A company of one hundred people does not need one hundred desks if, on a typical day, many of them are elsewhere. Nor does it need fewer if everyone comes in on the same two days each week. Headcount is a figure from the payroll. Attendance is a pattern of behaviour, and it is attendance the office must accommodate.

Planning from headcount alone tends to produce two problems at once: rows of empty desks on quiet days, and crowding on busy ones. The space feels underused and overfull in turn.

**What to do instead.** Before planning, observe how the current office is used. Badge records, room bookings and a simple count walked through the floor at intervals over a few weeks will show peak days, typical days and the pattern of meetings. Ask team leads which days their teams come in together. Plan the number of workpoints for a realistic peak, and use the space saved on quiet-day surplus for the settings people actually lack.

## Treating the building services as fixed background

A floor plan drawn without reference to the air conditioning, sprinklers, electrical risers and ceiling void is a drawing of an office that may not be buildable as shown. Rooms placed without regard to air-conditioning zones will be too hot or too cold. Partitions that cut across sprinkler coverage need new heads and fresh approvals. A low ceiling with a deep beam may leave no room for ducts where the plan needs them.

In Indian buildings, these constraints vary a great deal. A warm-shell floor in a tech park may have a central system designed for open plan; an older building may have split units mounted wherever convenient and a power supply with limited DG backup. Either way, the services shape the plan as firmly as the columns do.

**What to do instead.** Bring the services into the first layout. Survey what exists, ask the landlord for base-build drawings and capacities, and have a services engineer review the concept before it is approved. Changes to fire systems should be checked against the [National Building Code of India 2016](https://www.bis.gov.in/standards/technical-department/national-building-code/) and local fire rules at this stage, not after the ceiling is closed.

## Providing too few small rooms

Many offices are built with a large boardroom, a few eight-person meeting rooms and nowhere for one or two people to talk privately. Yet much of the working day now involves video calls, short conversations and interviews, none of which need a table for eight. The result is predictable: large rooms booked by single people, calls taken in stairwells and corridors, and noise spilling into the open floor.

**What to do instead.** Count the meetings that actually happen and their size. The count will often show that most involve only a few people. If it does, provide a generous number of small enclosed rooms and phone booths, built with proper acoustic separation, and fewer large rooms. Where a large room is needed occasionally, consider one that can be divided or formed by combining two medium rooms.

## Choosing finishes before understanding light

Materials are often selected from samples under a showroom's lighting, or from images on a screen. In the office itself, under daylight from a particular orientation and artificial light of a particular colour, the same finishes can look entirely different. A warm timber may turn orange; a cool grey may look lifeless; a gloss surface may reflect glare onto screens.

In a hot climate, the relationship between light and material matters further. A west-facing façade brings strong low sun in the afternoon, and pale reflective surfaces near the glass can make that harsher. Deep plans, far from windows, depend entirely on artificial light.

**What to do instead.** Study the daylight on the floor at different times before choosing finishes. Agree the lighting design, including colour temperature and the position of fittings, before the palette is fixed. Then review physical samples on site, in the actual light, beside each other, before approving them.

## Making no plan for maintenance

An office is designed once and maintained every day. Finishes that are hard to clean, light fittings that need specialist tools to reach, air-conditioning units without access panels, joinery with edges that chip under daily use: each becomes a recurring cost and a slow decline in appearance.

In Indian cities, dust, humidity and monsoon conditions add to the load. Pale fabrics near entrances darken quickly. Some timber products move in humid months. Exposed ceilings collect dust that is difficult to clean at height.

**What to do instead.** Involve the person who will run the office in design reviews. Ask, for each finish and fitting, how it is cleaned, how it is repaired and how long it is expected to last. Provide access to every service that will need attention. Specify finishes that can be patched or replaced in part rather than as a whole. Keep a small stock of spare tiles, carpet and paint for future repairs.

## The common thread

Each of these mistakes comes from making a decision without the information it needed. The remedy is not more design effort but earlier attention to the right questions: how people use the office, what the building can support, what the light does and who will look after it.
