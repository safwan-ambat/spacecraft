## The line between building and office

Every fit-out begins at a boundary. On one side is the base building: structure, façade, cores, lifts, main risers and the central plant that the landlord owns and maintains. On the other is the tenant's space, which the fit-out makes usable. The first task in any project is to establish exactly where that line falls in your lease, because it decides what you pay for, what you may alter and what you must return at the end of the term.

That line is rarely described in the same words twice. Leases, landlord manuals and contractors use overlapping terms, and the meaning shifts from one building to the next. It is worth learning the vocabulary, and then confirming what it means in your case.

## Shell and core, warm shell and the market's terms

**Shell and core** usually describes a floor delivered with structure, façade, cores, toilets, lifts and main services brought to the floor, but little or nothing within the tenant's area. The slab may be bare. There may be no ceiling, no lighting and no air conditioning distribution. Everything inside is the tenant's to provide.

**Warm shell** is a common Indian term for a floor that goes further. It may include a ceiling grid, base lighting, air conditioning to a standard layout, sprinklers and detection at regular spacing, and sometimes a raised floor or finished screed. The specification varies widely between developers.

You will also hear **Category A** and **Category B**, terms that originated in the United Kingdom and are used loosely in the Indian market. Category A generally means a landlord's standard finish, roughly comparable to a well-specified warm shell. Category B is the tenant's own fit-out: rooms, finishes, furniture, kitchens, branding and the adjustments to services that these require. Because usage is inconsistent, treat these labels as shorthand. The schedule of what is actually provided is what counts.

## The trades involved

A fit-out brings many trades into one space, often working at the same time. Broadly, they fall into a few groups:

- **Civil and dry trades:** demolition, masonry, drywall partitions, glazing, doors and frames.
- **Ceilings and floors:** grid or plasterboard ceilings, acoustic treatment, raised floors, carpet, vinyl, tile or stone.
- **Joinery and finishes:** built-in storage, reception counters, pantry units, paint, wall coverings and cladding.
- **Mechanical:** air conditioning, whether VRV, split units or extensions to a central chilled-water system, along with fresh air and exhaust.
- **Electrical:** distribution boards, lighting, small power, UPS and connection to DG backup.
- **Fire and life safety:** sprinklers, detection, alarms, emergency lighting and signage.
- **Plumbing:** pantry, wash areas and any additional toilets.
- **Low-voltage systems:** data cabling, Wi-Fi, access control, CCTV and audio-visual.
- **Furniture and loose items:** workstations, chairs, tables, storage and soft seating.

The sequence matters. Services above the ceiling must be complete and tested before the ceiling closes. Floors are finished after the heavier trades have left. Furniture arrives last. In Indian conditions, local trades are often skilled and quick, but lead times for imported items, specialist glass or system furniture can set the pace for everything else.

## Three ways to deliver a fit-out

**Design-bid-build** separates design from construction. A designer prepares drawings and specifications; contractors bid against them; the selected contractor builds. The client has independent design advice and a competitive price. The process is sequential, which can extend the programme, and gaps between the design and the bid can lead to variations.

**Design-and-build** places both under one contract. A single firm designs and constructs, and the stages can overlap. The client has one point of contact and a clearer line of responsibility. The trade-off is less independent scrutiny of cost and quality, which can be addressed through open-book pricing or a separate cost reviewer.

**PMC-led** delivery places a project management consultant at the centre. The PMC represents the client, coordinates designers and contractors, manages the programme and controls cost. This model suits larger or more complex projects and organisations without in-house capacity. It adds a layer of fees and a layer of oversight.

Many projects combine these. A client may appoint a designer and a PMC, then let packages to separate trade contractors. What matters is that every responsibility has one clear owner.

## Who obtains the approvals

A fit-out in India typically needs several permissions. The landlord or building management will want to review drawings, method statements and insurance before work starts. Fire services changes generally need to be designed in line with the [National Building Code of India 2016](https://www.bis.gov.in/standards/technical-department/national-building-code/) and approved or inspected according to local fire authority rules. Electrical changes may need certification. Some tech parks have their own review process and consultants.

Your contract should say who prepares each submission, who pays any fees or deposits and who carries the risk if an approval is delayed. Leaving this unstated is one of the most common causes of late handovers.

## Warranties and the period after handover

At handover, the office should arrive with a defects liability period, during which the contractor returns to correct faults. It should also come with manufacturers' warranties for equipment such as air-conditioning units, UPS systems and furniture, and with as-built drawings, test certificates and operation manuals.

Ask early how these will be collected and handed to you, and who you call when something fails in the first year. Treat this period as part of the project rather than the end of it. A fit-out is only complete when the people responsible for running the office know how it works.
