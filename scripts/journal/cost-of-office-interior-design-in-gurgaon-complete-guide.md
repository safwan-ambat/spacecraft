## Set the budget before the design

Many projects begin with a design and then ask what it costs. The order should be reversed. A budget set before design gives the designer a boundary to work within, and it forces the organisation to decide what the office is for before anyone draws it.

The starting figure does not need to be precise. It needs to be honest. It should come from the business case for the move: what the company can commit over the term of the lease, how long it expects to stay, and how much of the total it wishes to spend on the space itself rather than on furniture, technology or relocation. A number agreed by the people who will approve the final spend is worth more than a number borrowed from someone else's project.

## Feasibility: test the building against the figure

Before a lease is signed, there is a short window in which the building can still be changed. Use it. A feasibility study takes the proposed floor, the headcount and attendance pattern, and the budget, and asks whether the three can coexist.

In Gurugram, much of this rests on services. Many towers along the expressway corridors and in the newer sectors are handed over with base air conditioning and fire systems, but capacity and layout vary. Ask what cooling load the landlord's system can support, how much of the electrical load is on DG backup and whether there is space for additional UPS and battery rooms. In a region where summer heat is severe and winter mornings are cold, the ability of the building to keep the floor comfortable through both extremes is part of the cost, not an afterthought.

Air quality belongs in the same conversation. Outdoor pollution in the NCR rises sharply in the colder months, and public data from the [Central Pollution Control Board](https://airquality.cpcb.gov.in/AQI_India/) shows the pattern each year. Fresh-air handling, filtration and the space for it should be costed at feasibility, while the choice of building is still open.

## Design: let the estimate travel with the drawings

Once design begins, the budget should move alongside it. Each stage of drawing, from concept to detailed design, should be accompanied by an updated cost estimate. The estimate becomes more precise as the design does, and the gap between the two is where decisions are made.

If the concept estimate exceeds the budget, the choice is not simply to cut. It is to decide which parts of the brief carry the most value and which can be simplified, postponed or removed. This is far easier with a plan on paper than with walls already built. A useful habit is to record every such decision in a short register: what changed, why, and what it saved or added.

## Procurement: where decisions become commitments

Procurement converts the design into contracts and orders. It is also where timing begins to cost money. Items with long lead times, such as system furniture, specialist glazing, imported finishes or air-conditioning equipment, need to be ordered early. If the design is still moving when orders must be placed, the project either waits or pays for changes.

Two things protect the budget at this stage. First, freeze the parts of the design that drive long-lead orders, and resist revisiting them. Second, make sure tender documents are complete enough that bidders price the same scope. Gaps in a tender return later as variations, usually at a higher rate than they would have been priced at the outset.

## Build: protect the contingency

Every fit-out meets something unexpected: a beam lower than the drawing showed, a riser in poorer condition than reported, a change in the landlord's requirements. A contingency exists for these. It should be a separate line, held by the client rather than spread across the contractor's rates, and released only against a recorded reason.

The NCR adds its own risks. Construction activity in the region can be restricted during periods of poor air quality under the Graded Response Action Plan administered by the [Commission for Air Quality Management](https://caqm.nic.in/). How these orders apply to interior work varies with their wording at the time, so check the current position and allow for the possibility of pauses in dust-generating work. Extreme summer temperatures also affect labour productivity and the curing of some materials. Neither is a reason for alarm; both are reasons to keep the contingency intact until the end.

## Decisions have a cost curve

The same decision costs more the later it is made. Moving a meeting room on a concept plan costs a few hours of drawing. Moving it after the ceiling and sprinklers are installed costs demolition, rework, re-testing and time. This is the single most useful principle for anyone managing a fit-out budget.

It suggests a discipline. Agree the brief early and in full. Confirm services strategy before detailed design. Choose finishes and furniture before procurement, not during build. When a late change is unavoidable, price it before approving it, and decide whether it is worth what it costs.

## What to negotiate with the landlord

The fit-out budget is affected by the lease as well as the design. Landlords in Gurugram, as elsewhere, sometimes offer a rent-free period for fit-out, a contribution towards works, or the delivery of certain items as part of the base build. These are matters of negotiation, and their value depends on the market at the time and on the length of the commitment.

Treat them as part of the budget, not separate from it. A longer fit-out period may be worth more than a small contribution if approvals are slow. Base-build air conditioning delivered to your layout may be worth more than a credit against rent. Read a landlord's offer against the fit-out plan, not on its own: the cheapest office is the one whose costs were understood before the lease was signed.
