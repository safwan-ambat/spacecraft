## Why the unit matters

A floor of two hundred people may contain a dozen meeting rooms and one pantry, but it will contain two hundred workstations. Any decision about the desk is therefore repeated two hundred times. An extra few centimetres of width, a higher screen or a different power arrangement changes the capacity, the acoustics and the daylight of the entire floor.

This is why the workstation deserves to be designed as a unit before it is multiplied. Fix the module first, test it at full size, and the plan that follows will be more predictable. Leave it vague, and every later decision will be negotiated desk by desk.

## Choosing a configuration

Three arrangements cover most offices, each with its own character.

### Benching

Long, shared desks with people seated on both sides, divided by a central spine or screen. Benching is efficient with area, simple to extend and easy to reconfigure. It suits teams who work together closely and use mostly a laptop and one or two monitors. Its drawback is limited personal territory and more exposure to neighbouring conversation.

### L-shaped desks

A main worksurface with a return, usually arranged in clusters of two or four. The return gives space for paperwork, drawings or a second task, and the shape gives each person a clearer sense of their own corner. It uses more area per person and is harder to reconfigure, so it suits roles that genuinely need the extra surface.

### Pods

Small groups of desks, often three to six, arranged around a shared centre or in a cross. Pods give a team identity and keep everyone within speaking distance. They break up long rows, which helps with sightlines, but they can complicate circulation if the pod shape does not suit the column grid.

Typical desk widths in Indian offices fall roughly between 1,200 and 1,500 millimetres, with depths around 600 to 750 millimetres. Treat these as a working range to test against the actual tasks and equipment, not as a target.

## Power along a spine

How power and data reach the desk decides how flexible the floor will be. On a raised floor, floor boxes give freedom but fix positions once installed. On a solid slab, which is common in Indian commercial buildings, power usually arrives from the ceiling through poles or from the perimeter and the floor boxes provided at handover.

A shared spine running along the centre of a bench is often the cleanest answer. It carries a cable tray, power modules and data points for every seat, and it connects to the building supply at one or two points. Adding a desk becomes a matter of extending the spine.

Separate raw power from UPS power clearly. Computers and monitors should be on the UPS circuit; heaters, kettles and phone chargers should not. Label the sockets so that the distinction survives the first reorganisation.

## Screen height, light and sound

Desk screens have a stronger effect on the room than their size suggests. A low screen, at or slightly below seated eye level, keeps sightlines open, allows daylight from the façade to reach deeper into the plate and lets the room read as one space. A higher screen gives visual privacy and some acoustic separation, but it darkens the interior and makes the floor feel divided.

A common approach is to keep screens low along the direction of daylight and use slightly higher screens across it, between back-to-back seats. Absorbent materials on screens help with speech noise close to the desk; hard acrylic or glass screens reflect sound and do little acoustically.

## Storage per person

Storage is often over-provided out of habit. Many teams now keep little paper, and a full mobile pedestal under every desk takes leg room, collects clutter and adds to cleaning effort.

Ask each team what they actually store and where it needs to be. Personal items, a bag and a lunch box may need only a locker or a shallow shelf. Shared documents and samples belong in team storage near the group, not divided across every desk. Where pedestals are needed, choose a size that does not crowd the knee space, and remember that tall storage placed along a row works as a screen and affects light in the same way.

## Cables and the clean desk

Visible cable is a small irritation repeated many times. Plan a route for every cable from the spine or floor box to the device: a tray beneath the worksurface, a vertical trunking down the leg, a grommet or flap at the right point on the desk top. Leave capacity for one more cable than seems necessary.

Good cable management also makes cleaning easier, which matters in dusty conditions, and reduces the tangle of adaptors that grows under desks over time.

## The chair and the screen

The chair and monitor position matter more to daily comfort than the desk itself. A good task chair adjusts in seat height, seat depth, backrest angle and lumbar support, and it should be set up for each person rather than left at the factory setting.

General ergonomic guidance places the top of the monitor at or slightly below eye level, roughly an arm's length away, with the screen square to the user and away from direct glare from windows. Monitor arms make this easier to achieve across people of different heights. Laptop users benefit from a stand and a separate keyboard. Height-adjustable desks are worth considering for some seats, provided the power arrangement allows the desk to rise without pulling cables.

Settle these details on one sample workstation, sit at it for a day, and only then repeat it across the floor. Getting the unit right is a conversation worth having early.
