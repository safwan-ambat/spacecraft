## Read the building first

Every floor plate arrives with its own logic. The position of the core, the spacing of the columns, the depth from window to lift lobby and the line of the façade set the terms for everything that follows. A plan that ignores them will fight the building for as long as the lease runs.

Begin with a measured survey rather than the landlord's marketing drawing. Mark the columns, the shafts, the fire exits, the toilet blocks and any beams that drop below the ceiling line. Note where the air-conditioning units sit and where the electrical risers rise. In many Indian commercial buildings the handover condition varies widely: a bare shell may give you a raw slab and a single power point, while a warm shell may include a ceiling grid, toilets and base air conditioning. Know which one you have, because it changes what is fixed and what is still open.

Then walk the floor at different times of day. Light enters a north-facing façade evenly and a west-facing one with real force in the afternoon. The deepest parts of the plate, furthest from glass, will always be the dimmest. These observations do not appear on a drawing, but they decide where people will want to sit.

## Give daylight to the people who stay longest

The most valuable square metres in an office are the ones beside the window. A common habit is to give them to enclosed cabins, which places walls between the light and everyone else. The alternative is to keep the perimeter for open work settings and draw rooms towards the core, where they need artificial light in any case.

This is not a rule without exceptions. A west façade in a hot city may suit a meeting room used for an hour, with blinds drawn, better than a row of desks used all day. Storage, print areas and server rooms belong in the deep interior. The principle is simple: place each activity where its relationship with light is most comfortable, and resist the instinct to reward seniority with a view.

## Count activities, not headcount

Headcount tells you how many people the company employs. It does not tell you how many will be on the floor on a Tuesday, how many are on calls, or how many need to spread drawings across a table. Plans built on headcount alone tend to produce rows of identical desks and too few places to talk.

Before drawing anything, gather a week of honest information. Badge data, calendar bookings and a simple observation sheet walked twice a day will show the real rhythm of the office. Ask each team lead a few direct questions:

- How many people are in on your busiest day, and which day is it?
- How much of your team's time is spent on calls or video meetings?
- What do you store, and does it need to be near the desk?
- When do you work in groups, and how large are those groups?

The answers turn into a schedule of settings: a number of workpoints, a number of rooms by size, a quantity of quiet space and a quantity of shared space. That schedule, not the desk count, is the brief.

## Arrange the floor in zones of sound

Once the settings are known, group them by the noise they make and the quiet they need. Reception, the pantry and informal seating are naturally loud and belong together, close to the entrance. Focused work belongs furthest from them. Meeting rooms and project spaces can sit in between, acting as a buffer.

This gradient, from public and active at the door to private and calm at the far end, does more for concentration than any acoustic product added later. It also helps visitors: a guest can be welcomed, seated and taken to a meeting room without crossing the working floor.

## Draw the circulation before the desks

Circulation is often treated as the space left over once the furniture is placed. It should be the opposite. Draw the primary route first: from the entrance, past the shared spaces, to each fire exit, wide enough for two people to pass and for furniture to be moved in. The [National Building Code of India 2016](https://www.bis.gov.in/standards/technical-department/national-building-code/) and local fire authorities set requirements for exits and travel distances; your design team should confirm these against the building's approved plans before the layout is fixed.

A legible route also orients people. When the main path is clear and connects the places everyone uses, the floor feels calmer, and people find each other without effort.

## Leave room for the next change

No company stays the same size for the length of a lease. Plan for change at the outset. Keep meeting rooms on a regular module so that two small rooms can become one large one. Run power and data to the floor in a way that allows workstations to be reconfigured without breaking the ceiling. Choose a furniture system that can grow by adding desks to a spine rather than replacing it.

Some flexibility is simply restraint. A floor that is not filled to capacity on day one can absorb new joiners, a new team or a new way of working without a second fit-out.

## What to bring to the first meeting

A good space plan is a conversation between the building and the organisation. Arrive with a measured floor plan, the handover condition in writing, a week of occupancy observations and a short list of the activities that matter most to your teams. With those four things on the table, the first sketch can be about how people will work, rather than how many desks will fit.
