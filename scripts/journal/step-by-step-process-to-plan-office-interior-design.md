## A project is a chain of decisions

It is tempting to think of an office project as a series of deliverables: a plan, a set of renderings, a quotation, a finished floor. It is more useful to think of it as a series of decisions. Each stage exists to settle certain questions so that the next stage can rely on them. When a decision is reopened later, everything built upon it is disturbed.

The stages below follow a typical Indian office project from first conversation to the months after move-in. For each, it is worth asking three things: what must be decided, who decides it, and what should be frozen before moving on.

## Brief and survey

The brief records what the organisation needs. It covers headcount and attendance, the activities the office must support, the rooms and settings required, any technical needs such as server rooms or labs, the move-in date and the budget. It is written by the client, ideally with the help of the designer, and approved by whoever will sign off the final spend.

The survey records what the building provides. A measured drawing of the floor, the position of columns, shafts and exits, the ceiling height, the condition of existing services, the landlord's fit-out rules and the handover condition in writing. In a tech park this includes the fit-out manual; in an older building, it may include a frank look at the state of the risers and the power supply.

**Freeze before moving on:** the brief's essential requirements, the budget principle and an agreed set of base drawings.

## Concept

The concept stage tests how the brief fits the building. The designer produces a few layout options, each a different answer to the same question, along with an early sense of material and atmosphere. A first cost estimate should accompany them.

Decisions here are about arrangement rather than detail: where the entrance and reception sit, how teams are grouped, where meeting rooms and quiet rooms go, how circulation reaches the fire exits. The client chooses a direction, often combining parts of two options.

**Freeze before moving on:** the layout direction, the room schedule and the broad approach to services, such as whether air conditioning will be extended from the base build or installed new.

## Design development

Design development takes the chosen concept and makes it buildable. Partition types, ceiling design, lighting layouts, air conditioning zones, fire services changes, power and data positions, joinery and the materials palette are worked out in coordination. Furniture is selected. The cost estimate becomes a detailed one.

This is the stage where most choices about finish and specification are made, and where most budget adjustments should happen. A change on paper here costs time at a desk. The same change after procurement costs money and programme.

**Freeze before moving on:** the full layout, the services design, the finishes and furniture schedules, and a cost plan that matches the budget.

## Approvals and procurement

With design fixed, the project seeks permission and places orders. Landlord or building management approval, fire drawings in line with the [National Building Code of India 2016](https://www.bis.gov.in/standards/technical-department/national-building-code/) and the local fire authority, electrical approvals and any society or tech-park reviews proceed. Each has its own submission requirements and its own pace.

At the same time, tender documents go out or orders are placed. Long-lead items, such as system furniture, specialist glass, imported finishes and air-conditioning equipment, are committed first. The client approves contracts and purchase orders; the design team checks that what is ordered matches what was designed.

**Freeze before moving on:** approved drawings, signed contracts and confirmed orders for long-lead items.

## Build and snagging

Construction follows a sequence: demolition and preparation, services above the ceiling, partitions, ceilings, floors, joinery and finishes, then furniture. Weekly site meetings track progress, resolve questions and record decisions. Changes requested during construction should be priced and approved in writing before they are made.

As areas approach completion, the design team walks the space and lists defects: misaligned joints, damaged finishes, lights not working, doors that do not close properly. This is the snag list. The contractor closes each item, and the list is checked again.

**Freeze before moving on:** a closed snag list, test certificates for services and fire systems, and confirmation that approvals are in place for occupation.

## Handover and the first months

Handover is the transfer of the office from the project team to the people who will run it. It should include as-built drawings, warranties, operation manuals for equipment, maintenance contacts and a short walk-through for the facilities lead. Access cards, keys and building management registrations are completed.

The first months of use are part of the project. People will discover what works and what does not: a meeting room that is too warm in the afternoon, a pantry that is too small at lunch, a desk area that is noisier than expected. Keep a log of these observations, and agree in advance how minor adjustments will be made within the defects period. A review after a few months of occupation, comparing how the office is used with what the brief anticipated, is the most useful document a project can produce for the next one.

Whoever leads your project, the principle holds: decide each thing once, at the right time, and build on it with confidence.
