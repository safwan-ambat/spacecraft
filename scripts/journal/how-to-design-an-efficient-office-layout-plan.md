## A drawing is a hypothesis

The first layout for any floor is a guess made with good intentions. It looks resolved because the lines are clean and the desks repeat neatly, but it has not yet met a person carrying a laptop and a cup of tea, a chair pushed back from a desk, or a cleaner with a trolley. Until it has, it is a hypothesis.

Treating the plan this way changes the order of work. Instead of refining one scheme until it feels finished, the team produces several rough ones, tests each against the same questions, and only then develops the strongest. The time spent is small compared with the cost of discovering a fault after the partitions are up and the furniture has arrived from a factory several weeks away.

## Run two or three test fits

A test fit is a quick, honest layout that answers one question: does this brief fit on this floor, and how? Two or three are enough. More tend to blur into variations of the same idea.

Make each one genuinely different. One might keep every enclosed room against the core and leave the façade open. Another might cluster rooms into a band across the middle of the floor, dividing it into two quieter halves. A third might place the pantry at the window and push workstations inward. The point is to expose the trade-offs, not to find the answer on the first attempt.

Score every test fit against the same short list:

- How many workpoints, rooms and support spaces does it achieve against the brief?
- How long is the walk from the furthest desk to a toilet, a meeting room and a fire exit?
- Which settings receive daylight, and which sit in the deep plate?
- Where do the noisy and quiet activities touch?
- How much of the existing ceiling, air conditioning and electrical layout can stay?

The last question matters more in India than it is often given credit for. On a warm shell, relocating cassette units, VRV indoor units or sprinkler heads involves the landlord, the building management team and sometimes a revised fire drawing. A scheme that works with the services already installed is usually faster and cleaner to build.

## Draw everything at true scale

Many layouts fail because the furniture in the drawing is smaller than the furniture that arrives. Generic blocks from a software library rarely match the product being specified. Draw the real desk, the real chair in its pushed-back position, the real pedestal and the real storage unit with its doors open.

Then draw the things people forget. A chair needs room behind it to be pulled out and sat in, and the aisle behind a row of occupied chairs must still be passable. Doors swing. Cabinet drawers project. A column casing adds to the column. A floor box for power sits where it sits, not where the desk would like it. Columns are rarely on a perfect grid in older Indian buildings, so take dimensions from the measured survey rather than the landlord's base plan.

Print the plan at a true scale, lay a scale rule on it and check clearances by hand. Screens encourage zooming, and zooming hides tightness.

## Mark a typical cluster in tape

Once a preferred test fit emerges, take the most repeated element of the plan, usually a cluster of workstations with its aisle, and set it out on the actual floor in masking tape. Mark the desk edges, the chair zones, the aisle, any screen or storage line, and the nearest column. If a few sample desks and chairs can be borrowed, place them inside the tape.

Do the same for one meeting room of the most common size. Mark the walls, the door swing, the table and the chairs. A six-person room that seemed generous on paper can feel tight once a screen wall and a credenza are added, and the tape reveals it in minutes.

This step costs very little. It needs a roll of tape, a measuring tape, a printed plan and an afternoon on site before the contractor mobilises.

## Walk it as the people who will use it

A taped layout is only useful if it is walked with intent. Invite a few people who will actually sit there, not only the leadership team. Ask them to do ordinary things:

1. Sit at a desk and push the chair back as if standing up for a call.
2. Walk behind an occupied desk carrying a laptop and a mug.
3. Pass another person in the aisle.
4. Enter the meeting room, take the far seat and leave again while others are seated.
5. Stand where a screen will be and check the sightline from the door.

Note every moment of hesitation, every sideways step and every comment. Ask facilities and housekeeping to walk it too. They will see the cleaning routes, the bins, the water dispenser and the delivery path that designers tend to miss.

## Iterate, then fix the module

Take what the walk revealed back to the drawing. Often the change is small: a slightly wider aisle, one fewer desk per run, a door hinged on the other side, a room shifted to free a column. Because the tested cluster repeats across the floor, a small correction to the module improves the whole plan at once.

When the cluster and the typical room are settled, freeze them as the module. Every later decision, from ceiling layout to floor boxes to furniture orders, can then refer back to dimensions that have been tested in the real space. This is also the right moment to share the plan with the building management team and the fire consultant, since exit routes and travel distances under the [National Building Code of India 2016](https://www.bis.gov.in/standards/technical-department/national-building-code/) are easier to confirm on a plan that will not keep moving.

## What verification gives you

A tested layout is calmer to build. The contractor works from dimensions that have been checked. Furniture is ordered once. Teams move in to a floor they have already stood on. If you are about to commit to a plan, the useful question is not whether it looks right, but whether anyone has walked it yet. That is the question we like to begin with.
